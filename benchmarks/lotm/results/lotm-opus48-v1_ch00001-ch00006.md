## The three conditions

All three conditions share the same model, settings, prompt assembler, and style guide. Only the
context included in the prompt differs. None of the three was tuned to win; they are three points on
a "how much context" axis.

- **A - chapter-only.** The current source chapter plus the shared translation instructions and
  style guide. No prior translation history, no structured canonical state, no translation memory.
- **B - rolling context.** The current source chapter plus B's own previous translations (a rolling
  window). No structured canonical context, no translation memory.
- **C - Wenmai with persistent, human-reviewed memory.** The current source chapter plus C's own
  previous translations, plus bounded human-reviewed canonical state (characters, terminology,
  locations, factions, timeline) and bounded human-reviewed translation memory. Both the canonical
  state and the translation memory are chapter-bounded, so translating chapter *i* can only see
  material first established before chapter *i*.

Scoring is blind: candidates are presented as 1/2/3 and the label to condition map is withheld
until after scoring is frozen. Human scores are 1-5 (5 = best); `hallucination_or_embellishment`
uses 5 = no unsupported additions. Deterministic `fmt` = automated formatting validity (front
matter present + a single H1).

## Deterministic formatting

Formatting validity was independent of condition. The code-fence wrapping that invalidates a
candidate (the whole Markdown document emitted inside a code fence, so the front matter is not
recognized) appeared on different conditions each chapter: ch1 A; ch2 A and B; ch3 B and C; ch4 C;
ch5 none; ch6 none. It is a per-process output-formatting artifact of the current stateless CLI
backend, caught automatically, and deliberately left uncorrected so it stays a visible outcome. It
does not track translation quality (in ch3 and ch4 the highest-scored candidate was flagged). No
candidate in any chapter used a banned terminology variant (`banned_variant_count` was 0
throughout).

## Informative sample and headline

Chapter 1 is non-informative (equivalent prompts). The meaningful experimental sample through this
checkpoint is **chapters 2-6, n = 5 informative chapters**.

- **C first: 5/5** informative chapters.
- **B over A: 4/5** (chapters 2, 3, 4, 6).
- **A over B: 1/5** (chapter 5).

Five out of five is an encouraging repeated signal, not statistical proof and not evidence of
general superiority. With N = 5, a single run, one evaluator family, and ordinal scores, this has no
statistical power; it is a directional early result.

### Narrative continuity vs translation-state continuity

It helps to separate two things a translator has to keep consistent:

- **Narrative continuity**: understanding what is happening in the story (who is who, what just
  happened, what a scene refers to). This is what `contextual_correctness` mostly measures.
- **Translation-state continuity**: rendering the same source item the same way every time it
  appears (the frozen `Kingdom of Loen`, `fortune-changing ritual`, `crimson stars`, `Sequence 9`).
  This is what `terminology_consistency` mostly measures.

By chapters 5-6, all three conditions preserved the broad narrative context well enough to receive
maximum `contextual_correctness` scores, including chapter-only (A), which has no history at all.
The larger and more persistent difference was in terminology. Chapter-only translation kept
drifting on established renderings; rolling context reduced but did not eliminate that drift; the
human-reviewed persistent-memory condition preserved the frozen terminology decisions throughout the
informative sample. This is an interim observation from this run, not a universal claim.

## Where C did not dominate

C did not win every dimension, and this matters for reading the result honestly.

- **Character voice**: B tied C on the mean (both 4.4).
- **Wordplay**: B tied C on the mean (both 4.2).
- **Annotation restraint**: all three conditions had a perfect mean (5.0).
- **Hallucination / embellishment**: B had the *best* mean (4.8), ahead of C (4.6) and A (4.4). More
  state did not make C the most restrained; C showed occasional unsupported embellishment despite
  its richer context.
- **Chapter 5 ranked C > A > B**, so B is not mechanically ahead of A.
- Several individual chapters separated C and B by only one point on the mean.

C leads on the aggregate and on the two continuity dimensions, but it is not uniformly better across
the board.

## Fresh-evaluator protocol (from chapter 4 onward)

Starting with chapter 4, each chapter was evaluated in a **fresh, independent evaluator session**.
Chapters 4, 5, and 6 were scored this way. Each fresh evaluator received only:

- the fixed evaluation rubric,
- the current Chinese source chapter,
- the blinded Candidate 1/2/3 translations,
- the deterministic result,
- a blank evaluation schema,
- the canonical state established *before* that chapter (needed to judge longitudinal consistency).

Each fresh evaluator was deliberately **not** given: prior candidate to condition mappings, previous
rankings, any statement of which condition was expected to do best, previous evaluator comments, or
previous candidate-number patterns. External or reference LOTM material was prohibited.

This was introduced to reduce evaluator expectation bias. A useful piece of evidence: the winning
**candidate number** changed across the three fresh sessions (chapter 4: candidate 3; chapter 5:
candidate 1; chapter 6: candidate 2), yet all three independently unblinded to Condition C. That
argues against a trivial fixed-candidate-number preference, though it does not eliminate all
evaluator bias. Chapters 2-3 were *not* run under this fresh-session protocol and should not be
described as if they were.

## What the state-extraction audit is teaching us

Persistent memory introduces its own failure surface. The automatic context extractor can convert
plausible but unsupported model "knowledge" into durable future context, which would then influence
every later chapter. In this run, human review repeatedly caught that before it entered canonical
state. Summarized without reproducing any source passages:

- **Chapter 1**: no meaningful proposal-leakage issue identified.
- **Chapter 2**: one explicit future-story / model-prior claim was proposed inside a source term's
  explanation. Human review rejected it before it entered canonical state; the frozen chapter-2
  translation itself did not contain the leakage.
- **Chapter 3**: the seven orthodox deities initially looked suspicious but were verified as
  source-grounded (the chapter lists all seven explicitly). The real issues were unsupported
  inference and over-generalization: a standalone `Backlund` location inferred from
  `Backlund University`; two first-observed phrases asserted to be already recurring / running
  jokes; an incorrect proposed `first_seen` for `staff sergeant`; and an attempt to introduce a new
  top-level `worldbuilding` category. These were rejected or corrected.
- **Chapter 4**: Roselle Gustav's large historical block was verified as source-grounded, not
  latent-pretraining leakage. Review nevertheless removed an unsupported `Ancient Feysac` to
  `Feysac Empire` relationship, an invented `pound sterling` avoid variant, and claims that certain
  references were already recurring motifs.
- **Chapter 5**: the Audrey / Alger / gray-fog material was source-grounded. Review removed semantic
  upgrades: treating the bronze mirror as inherited, upgrading the Black Emperor description beyond
  the chapter, treating a glass bottle as a known concoction, and formalizing an unsupported
  theological rank hierarchy for the ritual invocation. It also exercised the delayed-promotion
  policy: an exact earlier phrase was promoted to translation memory only after an actual second
  occurrence was observed, rather than accepting an extractor-invented generalized template.
- **Chapter 6**: the Sequence / potion material was source-grounded. The extractor nevertheless
  proposed a new top-level `power_system` schema/category, invented potion avoid variants, unsupported
  church affiliations for named groups, and premature classification of a maxim as recurring. Human
  review kept the source-grounded terminology (represented in the existing schema) while rejecting
  the schema change and the unsupported relationships.

The pattern across six chapters: human review is doing more than polishing extracted memory. In this
run it has repeatedly prevented plausible but unsupported information from becoming persistent
context. This suggests persistent memory should be treated as a curated state-management problem, not
merely an extraction problem. Again, this is an observation from the current run.

## Interim conclusions

**A. Structured memory currently shows a repeatable advantage.** Condition C finished first in all
five informative chapters. This is an encouraging repeated signal, not statistical proof.

**B. The strongest advantage appears to be translation-state continuity.** The clearest sustained
separation is terminology consistency. Rolling context substantially improves contextual correctness
over isolated translation but does not eliminate terminology drift.

**C. Rolling context is genuinely strong.** B is not a straw-man baseline. By chapters 4-6 it often
matched C on broad contextual correctness and sometimes on voice and wordplay.

**D. Chapter-only translation remains surprisingly capable.** A reached maximum contextual-correctness
scores in chapters 5-6 despite having no chapter history, which likely reflects how much the
immediate source chapter plus a capable pretrained model can accomplish. The cause should not be
attributed more strongly than the evidence permits.

**E. More memory is not the same as safer output.** B currently has the best
hallucination/embellishment mean, and C has shown occasional unsupported embellishment despite its
richer state.

**F. Human-reviewed memory is empirically justified within this run.** Multiple extractor proposals
contained unsupported, premature, or model-prior-derived material that would have contaminated future
state if accepted automatically.

**G. The benchmark is unfinished.** **6 / 10 chapters complete; 5 informative chapters evaluated.**
Chapters 7-10 could weaken, reverse, or complicate these patterns.

At the six-chapter checkpoint, the evidence does not support the simple claim that more context
always produces better translation. It supports a narrower and more useful one: rolling context is
often enough to recover narrative continuity, while explicit human-reviewed state appears better at
preserving translation decisions across chapters. That benefit carries a matching responsibility,
because an unsupported inference stored once can influence every chapter that follows. No claim of
statistical significance is made, and nothing here is claimed to generalize beyond this corpus, this
language pair, this model/backend, and this benchmark protocol.

## Methodological limitations

- One novel / corpus.
- Chinese to English only.
- One primary model / backend (`claude-opus-4-8`, `claude_code_stateless`).
- Only five informative chapters so far.
- Ordinal, human-assisted scores.
- Evaluator sessions use language models and therefore cannot fully eliminate latent-pretraining
  knowledge.
- The source novel is well known enough that model-weight familiarity with it cannot be ruled out.
- Fresh evaluator sessions (from chapter 4) reduce but do not eliminate evaluator bias.
- Condition C includes human review, so this benchmark measures the full reviewed-memory workflow,
  not purely automatic retrieval.

The official English translation remains withheld from all prompting and evaluation until the full
ten chapters are complete; it is an after-the-fact comparison aid only.
